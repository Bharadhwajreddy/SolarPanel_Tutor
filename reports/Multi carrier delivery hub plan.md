# Stacking carriers works, but slowly and conditionally

A multi-carrier delivery hub in Hyderabad can work. It works as a slow, staged build, though, not as a quick win. Carrying parcels for many companies at once does not fix the core problem found in the earlier single-carrier analysis: after rider pay, the hub keeps only a few rupees per delivered parcel. In my base case the hub keeps **about ₹3.50 per last-mile parcel**, against **₹27 on a walk-in courier booking** and **₹18 on a pick-pack order**. Doing several things under one roof is what makes money. Last-mile parcels bring in the volume, and the counter, seller shipping accounts and fulfilment cover the fixed costs. My 24-month model is my arithmetic on sourced inputs plus clearly labelled assumptions. It gives three results. In the **base case** (₹19 blended payout per parcel, riders at ₹14.50, 480 parcels a day by month 24) the hub has its first profitable month in the October festive peak of year 1 and is **profitable every month from month 19**. It needs **about ₹11.5 lakh of cash at its lowest point** (month 21). That cash goes mostly into deposits and unpaid carrier invoices, not losses, and it takes **about four years to pay back**. The **optimistic case** (₹21 payout, 30% more volume) needs only **about ₹3.9 lakh** and pays back in **17 months**. The **pessimistic case** (₹16 payout) never breaks even. Last-mile work earns nothing at that rate, so the plan includes stop rules that limit the loss to about ₹3 lakh. Volume is not the problem: Hyderabad moves an estimated half-million e-commerce parcels a day. The real dangers are the payout spread, carrier concentration and slow payment. Ecom Express and Dunzo's vendors show how those kill delivery businesses. My verdict is to go ahead **only** after one carrier puts a rate of ₹19 or more and its PIN-code volume in writing, with a second carrier from a different parent group lined up before the first festive season, and with access to roughly ₹11-12 lakh released in three tranches.

## How the business actually works: you rent out riders and space, and own only the counter customer

A multi-carrier hub is one rented shop-front unit in a dense Hyderabad suburb. Several logistics companies drop their parcels there each morning, and your riders deliver them. Each company pays you a fixed amount for each delivered parcel. They do not pay you a share of the shipping price. The companies own the customer, set the rate and decide how many parcels you get. Valmo's published agreement shows how lopsided this is. You work as an "independent contractor". Valmo can end the arrangement "with or without cause" on **3 working days' notice**. It can change fees and terms, and "continued usage" counts as your acceptance. It can also deduct penalties, unremitted cash-on-delivery (COD) money and the value of lost parcels from what it pays you ([Valmo delivery agreement](https://www.valmo.in/static-assets/valmo-partner-app/documents/valmo-app-delivery-service-agreement-v1.0.html)). That document is the rider-level agreement, and the hub ("Captain") contract is not public. Plan on the hub terms being similar.

The streams you *do* control are those where you serve a customer directly. These are a walk-in courier counter, shipping accounts for local online sellers (booked through aggregators such as Shiprocket), and pick-pack fulfilment. They earn several times more per unit. The plan therefore treats last-mile delivery as the volume engine that fills the riders' day and attracts footfall, and the direct-customer streams as the profit engine.

**Can you legally serve several carriers at once?** Nothing published says you can't. A full-text search of Valmo's agreement finds no exclusivity, non-compete or non-solicit clause ([Valmo agreement](https://www.valmo.in/static-assets/valmo-partner-app/documents/valmo-app-delivery-service-agreement-v1.0.html)). Shadowfax gives its delivery partner an exclusive PIN-code area. That protects you from competitors inside Shadowfax's network; it does not tie you to Shadowfax ([FranchiseIndia, third-party](https://www.franchiseindia.com/brands/shadowfax-technologies-private-limited.57551)). Multi-carrier vendors already exist in Hyderabad. A rider-job listing in Nizampet describes Shubankara Logistics as a vendor for both Ekart and Shadowfax ([WorkIndia](https://www.workindia.in/jobs/delivery_boy-nizampet-hyderabad-8504350/)). However, no public case study was found of a small operator who grew from a few riders to 20-100 across several carriers, and the hub contracts of Shadowfax, XpressBees, Ekart, Delhivery and Amazon are not public. Treat multi-carrier work as allowed **unless the contract you are handed says otherwise**, and read every clause on "conflict of interest", "competing business" and "dedicated premises" before signing. Amazon Hub Delivery terms in particular may restrict deliveries for competing platforms from the same store. This was not verified, so ask Amazon directly.

In practice, each carrier expects to be kept separate from the others. Each issues its own app login for every rider, tied to that rider's personal KYC. Valmo requires COD cash to be deposited the **same day** "as a bailee and agent" ([Valmo agreement](https://www.valmo.in/static-assets/valmo-partner-app/documents/valmo-app-delivery-service-agreement-v1.0.html)). The safe set-up is separate rack zones, separate COD cash boxes and ledgers, and CCTV covering each zone. Riders should be dedicated to one carrier unless the agreements allow multiple IDs. One cost of this separation is that serving many carriers does **not** make each rider's route denser; that would need riders to carry mixed loads. The real gain from several carriers is spreading rent, supervision and equipment over more parcels, plus protection when one client walks away.

## From application to first payment takes six to ten weeks per carrier

No company publishes an end-to-end timeline. The only benchmarks are **12-15 days** for Shadowfax franchise onboarding (third-party, older) ([FranchiseIndia](https://www.franchiseindia.com/brands/shadowfax-technologies-private-limited.57551)) and **3-5 business days** for an Amazon Hub Delivery application decision in the US ([Amazon Hub guide, US](https://partner-essentials.hub-delivery.amazon.com/guide/what-youll-need-to-you-apply)). Approval is driven by demand: a carrier onboards a partner when a PIN code is under-served, not in order of application. The path from first parcel to first money depends on the payment cycle. Amazon's official page says Hub Delivery partners are paid weekly ([About Amazon India](https://www.aboutamazon.in/news/operations/i-have-space-discover-an-extra-source-of-income-with-zero-investments)), while a Shiprocket blog says payment arrives "by the 1st week of every month" ([Shiprocket](https://www.shiprocket.in/blog/amazon-i-have-space-program/)). Shadowfax, a listed company, took **36-48 days** on average to collect its own invoices ([Screener](https://www.screener.in/company/id/1285868/consolidated)). Expect your first carrier payment 30-45 days after your first delivery.

The same nine steps apply to almost every carrier.

| Step | What happens | Typical time (where known) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Apply | Online form, partner app or email (channels below) | Same day |
| 2. Call from city or area operations team | They ask about your PIN codes, space, riders and background | Days to weeks; depends on whether they need a partner in your area |

Prepare one "partner pack" that works for every carrier. Pay a deposit only into a company account, against a corporate-domain agreement.

### GST and TDS, explained with one invoice

Delivery work is a courier service under **SAC 9968 (996812), taxed at 18% GST** under the normal forward charge. It is not the 5%/12% goods-transport (GTA) rate, a common mistake ([ClearTax](https://cleartax.in/s/postal-courier-services-gst-rates-sac-code-9968); [TaxClue](https://taxclue.in/guide/gst-on-courier)). Registration is mandatory in Telangana only above ₹20 lakh of turnover ([TaxGuru](https://taxguru.in/?p=1069174)). Register from day one anyway: Valmo makes GST compliance "the sole responsibility of the User" ([Valmo T&C](https://www-int.valmo.in/static-assets/valmo-partner-app/documents/valmo-app-terms-and-conditions-v3.0.html)), XpressBees reportedly asks for an active GST certificate, and my base case passes ₹20 lakh of annualised revenue by month 6 in any case. Carriers deduct income-tax TDS under section 194C. The rate is **1% for an individual or proprietorship, 2% for a firm or company, and 20% without a PAN**. It applies above ₹30,000 for a single payment or ₹1 lakh a year ([Teachoo](https://teachoo.com/949/367/Section-194C-TDS-on-Contractors/category/Rates-of-TDS); [Sortingtax](https://sortingtax.com/tds-on-courier-service/)). The new Income-tax Act took effect on 1 April 2026, and the section numbering may have changed. Confirm it with a CA.

Here is a worked example (my arithmetic). Suppose Carrier A's statement shows 4,000 delivered parcels at ₹19. You invoice ₹76,000 plus ₹13,680 GST, a total of ₹89,680. The carrier deducts about ₹760 of TDS (1%) and transfers about ₹88,900. You file GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B monthly and pay the ₹13,680, less input credit on equipment and aggregator freight. The GST is often due **before the carrier has paid you**, which is one reason the business needs working capital. The ₹760 of TDS is not a cost. It appears in your Form 26AS/AIS and is credited against your income tax. Two related warnings. Walk-in counter customers must also be charged GST once you are registered, so unregistered rival counters can undercut you by about 18%. And COD cash is the carrier's money: deposit it daily and keep it outside your service invoices.

## Demand is not the bottleneck: Hyderabad moves about half a million parcels a day

India shipped **4.9-5.3 billion e-commerce parcels in FY25** (about 14 million a day, excluding grocery and quick commerce). Volume grew 28-31% a year over FY20-25, and Redseer projects **23-27% a year** growth to 15-16 billion by FY30 ([Redseer report in Shadowfax IPO documents](https://www.shadowfax.in/investor-relations/offer-documents/shadowfax_india_3rd_party_logistics_market_industry_report.pdf)). No source gives Hyderabad's share. Assuming 3-4% of national volume (my assumption) puts the city at roughly **0.5-0.7 million parcels a day in FY26**. A hub doing 480 a day would handle under 0.1% of that. Hyderabad also has **484 quick-commerce dark stores, the third-most in India**, behind Bengaluru (735) and Delhi (531) ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/corporate/flipkart-minutes-overtakes-swiggy-instamart-in-dark-stores-across-top-10-cities)). It serves as the sorting base for both Telangana and Andhra Pradesh: Delhivery runs hubs at Shamshabad and Medchal ([Delhivery call transcript](https://www.delhivery.com/uploads/2023/11/EarningsCallTranscript_Q2FY24.pdf)). So the limit on your volume is **how many PIN codes carriers assign you**, not how many parcels the market holds.

Who carries the parcels matters for whom you apply to. Captive networks (Ekart, Amazon, Valmo) moved **about 58-60%** of e-commerce shipments in FY25, and third-party carriers about 40-42%. Among third-party carriers, **Delhivery (now including Ecom Express) holds about 46% and Shadowfax about 23%** ([Redseer](https://www.shadowfax.in/investor-relations/offer-documents/shadowfax_india_3rd_party_logistics_market_industry_report.pdf)). Valmo is the fastest mover. It carried **48% of Meesho's orders in FY25 and 64.5% in H1 FY26**, and handled 763 million shipments in FY25 ([Axis Capital note](https://bsmedia.business-standard.com/_media/bs/data/market-reports/equity-brokertips/2026-01/17679504020.34145800.pdf)). A brokerage gives a different split, with Amazon and Ekart at 33% each and Delhivery at 20% of express parcels ([Equitec note](https://bsmedia.business-standard.com/_media/bs/data/market-reports/equity-brokertips/2025-09/17567186470.65777600.pdf)). The share estimates in the appendix are therefore ranges converted into single numbers so they can be charted. For a small hub, the carriers that actually outsource the last mile to local partners are Shadowfax, Valmo (an asset-light partner network by design), Delhivery, XpressBees, and Amazon through Hub Delivery.

Seasonality is sharp. Delhivery's quarterly volumes for FY26 ran in a ratio of 100 : 119 : 142 from April-June to October-December. In October 2025 it moved **107 million shipments, with a single-day peak of 7.2 million** ([Delhivery Q3 FY26](https://www.delhivery.com/uploads/2026/01/EarningsPresentation_Q3FY26.pdf); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/delhivery-moves-over-19-000-crore-in-goods-amid-record-festive-surge-125110301088_1.html)). The research notes scale these ratios into a monthly curve, which is an inference since no Indian monthly series is published. February is the low point at 88, and October peaks at 140. That makes October about 1.6 times February, with peak *days* at 2-3 times the average. The first week of the 2026 festive sales saw e-commerce orders rise **31%** and quick-commerce orders **85%** year on year ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/industry/news/ecom-order-volumes-rise-31-in-first-week-of-festive-sales-unicommerce-126101401136_1.html)). Returns follow 1-3 weeks behind the peak. One brand set's D2C return-to-origin rate hit **39.2% in November 2025**, falling to 21% by March 2026 ([Unicommerce](https://unicommerce.com/india-d2c-report-2026-april/)). Nationally, COD is about 60-65% of orders ([The Growth Agency](https://www.thegrowthagency.in/blogs/cod-rto-cost-india)). In a metro like Delhi it is 40%, with a 12% return-to-origin rate ([Shiprocket Trends](https://trends.shiprocket.in/)). For Hyderabad, assume 40-50% COD, which means a lot of daily cash to control. This seasonality is why the plan launches in February: the slow months allow a gentle learning curve, and both carriers should be live before the September-October peak, when carriers lean hardest on local partners.

Last-mile delivery is a pass-through business. In the base case riders take ₹14.50 of every ₹19, about 76%. That matches what the filings show. Shadowfax, at a scale of ₹1,806 crore of half-year revenue, spends about 53% of revenue on delivery staff, about 8% on lost shipments, and keeps an **EBITDA margin of 3.56%** ([Entrackr](https://entrackr.com/fintrackr/shadowfax-reports-rs-1806-cr-revenue-in-h1-fy26-profits-double-10613200)). A sub-hub sits further down the chain and can only keep less. Two thoughts follow. The **order** in which streams are stacked should follow the capital needed and the margin. And the high-margin direct-customer streams should be added as early as the space allows.

The recommended order runs as follows. **First**, on day one, start the near-zero-capital streams: an aggregator account and an Ekart booking wallet for walk-ins (no deposit), plus Amazon Hub Delivery if Amazon accepts your counter as a store (no investment, 20-60 packages a day at its official partner stores) ([About Amazon India](https://www.aboutamazon.in/news/operations/i-have-space-store-owners-recount-their-success-stories)). **Second**, sign one anchor last-mile contract: whichever of Shadowfax, Valmo, XpressBees or Delhivery confirms your PIN codes and rate in writing first. **Third**, by month 6 and before the festive season, add a second carrier from a *different parent group*, and start signing local sellers for shipping accounts. **Fourth**, in year 2, add a third carrier and pick-pack fulfilment once sellers commit volume. Porter vehicles, EV leasing and quick-commerce rider supply stay out of the base plan. None has a published vendor rate, and in November 2025 Zepto and Instamart cut rider earnings per order from ₹22-30 to **₹15-27** within weeks ([Financial Express](https://www.magzter.com/en/stories/newspaper/Financial-Express-Chandigarh/FEE-CUT-PAIN-FOR-DELIVERY-PARTNERS)). These are options to price later with real quotes. Do not build the plan on them.

## The plan: four phases, with about ₹11.5 lakh of cash at the low point, mostly working capital

The required investment is **derived from the monthly cash model**, not assumed. It is the deepest point of the cumulative cash line: everything spent on equipment, deposits, losses and unpaid invoices before cash starts coming back. Only part of this is money lost. In the base case, about ₹2.3 lakh is refundable deposits and about ₹5.2 lakh is unpaid carrier invoices plus GST paid before collection. That money comes back as the business slows or closes, and the unpaid-invoice part also shrinks after each festive peak.

Rent and equipment figures come from the earlier Hyderabad research. Small units list at ₹6,000-18,000 a month, for example a 520 sq ft unit on Kompally Road at ₹18,000 ([NoBroker](https://www.nobroker.in/rent-commercial-shops-in-kompally_hyderabad)), and the core sorting kit costs roughly ₹55,000-75,000 before a computer, furniture and signage. Warehousing in Hyderabad averages about **₹20.8 per sq ft a month** ([RP Realty Plus/Vestian](https://www.rprealtyplus.com/news-views/indias-warehousing-absorption-falls-14-in-2025-despite-strong-growth-123899.html)), which supports the phase-3 rent assumption. Carrier deposits are the biggest unknown: I use ₹50,000 per carrier, between Ekart's official zero and the ₹1-3 lakh claimed by third-party sites for Delhivery. In the other scenarios the low point is **₹3.9 lakh (optimistic**, deposits at zero) and about ₹5.8 lakh by month 5 in the **pessimistic** case, where the stop rule should end it.

The release of each tranche depends on a gate, and each gate is a number you can check.

| Gate | When | Pass only if | Base case | Pessimistic case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gate 0 | Around day 60, before signing the lease | At least one carrier gives in writing a blended rate of ₹19 or more and at least 100 parcels a day for named PIN codes; a second carrier from another group is in active talks; deposit and refund terms are written down; no exclusivity clause | Pass | Fails on rate (₹16); this gate should stop it before any lease |
| Gate 1 | End of month 5, before the second deposit and the supervisor hire | Average monthly loss over months 3-5 under ₹20,000 and shrinking; carrier payments arrive within 45 days; debits under ₹1.50 a parcel | Pass (average loss ₹13,554) | Fail (average loss ₹32,961) |
| Gate 2 | End of month 12, before expanding | Average monthly loss over months 10-12 under ₹15,000; a third carrier, or at least three sellers committing to fulfilment volume, is ready | Narrow pass (average loss ₹10,802) | Not reached |

The base case passes all three gates, though only narrowly at Gate 2. The pessimistic case should be stopped at Gate 0, because the ₹16 rate would already be in writing. If the rate falls only after you sign, Gate 1 catches it. The pessimistic case fails Gate 1. If you stop there, about ₹5.8 lakh has gone out. You get back about ₹1.45 lakh in deposits and about ₹89,000 in outstanding invoices, plus whatever the equipment resells for, so the **net loss is roughly ₹3 lakh**. Without the gates, the pessimistic case would burn ₹20 lakh by month 24.

Headcount grows slowly because riders are paid per parcel and scale with volume. In the base case it runs from the owner alone in phase 0, to 3-6 people in phase 1, 8-13 in phase 2 and 11-18 in phase 3 at the festive peak (13 riders in October 2028). Riders at ₹14.50 a parcel earn about **₹17,000-21,000 a month** at 45-55 deliveries a day. That is above Telangana's Zone I minimum wage of **₹16,000** for unskilled workers ([HRengage](https://hrengage.ai/compliance/minimum-wages/telangana)). In the pessimistic case, riders at 40 a day fall below it, which is a legal risk if they are treated as employees. Salaried staff pay 3.25% employer ESI ([FactoHR](https://factohr.com/?p=49132)). Plan for EPF once you reach 20 workers, a limit that counts per-parcel riders if they are classed as employees. Rider churn is high: Shadowfax had over 2 million registered partners but only about 1.5 lakh active in a month ([StartupTalky](https://startuptalky.com/shadowfax-success-story/)). The model therefore budgets for replacing 10% of riders every month at ₹1,500 each.

The business is **profitable every month from month 19 (August 2028)**. Cash is at its lowest at **−₹11.45 lakh in month 21**, the October 2028 peak, when receivables are largest. To estimate payback I extended the model beyond month 24 at 10% annual volume growth, an assumption. On that basis, cumulative cash turns positive in about **month 48 (early 2031)**, or month 46 if refundable deposits are excluded. Year 1 loses ₹1.69 lakh, year 2 makes ₹1.24 lakh, and year 3 makes about ₹4.8 lakh, an average of ₹40,000 a month before you take any pay. In the **optimistic** case the hub is profitable from **month 4** and pays back in **month 17 (June 2028)**. ## Operators fail from client collapse and slow payment, not bad riding

The best-documented failures in Indian delivery are about who the client is, not about how well the riders ride. **Ecom Express** let one client, almost certainly Meesho, grow from 29% to **52% of revenue**. Then Meesho built Valmo, cut Ecom's volume by about 40-50% by December 2024, and Ecom shut **about 1,000 delivery centres** before selling to Delhivery in a distress sale ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/features/what-triggered-ecom-express-165-mn-fire-sale-to-delhivery); [Delhivery](https://www.delhivery.com/press-release/delhivery-acquires-ecom-express)). Its partner franchise schemes reportedly ended with the sale ([Linkdot](https://linkdot.io/ecom-express-franchise-cost/)). **Dunzo** left its suppliers unpaid. Betterplace, which recruited and verified Dunzo's delivery staff, filed an insolvency claim for **about ₹4 crore** ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/buzz/nclt-warns-dunzo-of-moratorium-over-unpaid-dues-worth-inr-4-cr)). Other vendors had sold their Dunzo invoices to financiers to bridge the cash gap, and those financiers then went to court ([Bar & Bench](https://www.barandbench.com/amp/story/news/litigation/nclt-dismisses-insolvency-plea-by-invoice-discounters-against-dunzo)). Even the winners are concentrated. Shadowfax earned **48.9% of its revenue from Flipkart** in H1 FY26 and 74.6% from its top five clients, and lists this as its top risk ([INDmoney](https://www.indmoney.com/blog/ipo/shadowfax-ipo-review)). Scale does not guarantee survival either. Loadshare's revenue fell 12% in FY24 ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/company/loadshare/financials/)), and Zypp lost ₹107.5 crore on ₹438 crore of FY25 revenue ([Entrackr](https://entrackr.com/fintrackr/zypp-electric-revenue-grows-50-in-fy25-losses-stands-at-rs-107-cr-11126259)).

These cases give the multi-carrier plan its concentration rule. In year 1, expect one anchor carrier to dominate; the model has it at 49% of revenue in month 3. Bring it **below 40% by month 6-12 and below 30% in year 2**. The model reaches 31% at month 12 and 20% at month 24. **Count related clients as one exposure.** Flipkart is Shadowfax's investor and top client, so Ekart and Shadowfax together are effectively Flipkart exposure. Valmo is Meesho. These thresholds are inferred from the Ecom Express case, not taken from any formal standard. The documented growth stories are slow, too. Amazon's own success stories describe one delivery-partner couple growing from one station in 2015 to 45 stations in 21 cities, and a Kerala partner reaching six stations with about 300 staff in about five years ([About Amazon India](https://www.aboutamazon.in/news/operations/meet-the-power-couple-running-45-delivery-stations-in-21-cities-across-north-india); [About Amazon India](https://www.aboutamazon.in/news/operations/this-amazon-delivery-services-partner-is-creating-jobs-in-kerala)). Amazon's partner count stayed **around 300 from 2021 to 2023** ([IndiaRetailing](https://www.indiaretailing.com/?p=169909)), so new places are rare. A full Amazon delivery-partner station usually serves Amazon only, which makes it a later, separate venture rather than part of this hub.

The one first-hand small-operator story in the earlier research is a Valmo hub that received **20-25 shipments a day** against fixed rent and then closed ([Medium](https://medium.com/@swapnashupradhan5492/the-harsh-reality-behind-my-valmo-franchise-success-story-what-they-dont-tell-you-4ad1a5c93caf)). It is the clearest reason Gate 0 requires written PIN-code volume before you sign a lease.

| Risk | How it shows up | How to manage it |
|---|---|---|
| Thin or falling payout | Carrier revises rates "from time to time"; the last-mile spread falls toward zero | Pay riders per parcel so cost falls with volume; keep the lease lock-in short; build counter and seller streams to at least 50% of contribution; kill rule at Gate 1 |
| Concentration | One carrier above 40%, or two carriers from the same group | Second carrier from a different group by month 6; third by month 13; track share monthly |
| Slow payment and working capital | 30-60 day collections, GST due before cash arrives | Hold 1.5-2 months of payroll as cash; reconcile weekly; invoice on day 1 of the month; avoid depending on invoice discounting |
| Client collapse or in-sourcing | A carrier exits, merges or takes routes in-house (Ecom Express) | Keep deposits recoverable; know each contract's notice terms; no carrier-specific big capex |
| Debits, losses and COD | Lost parcels charged at invoice value; COD shortfalls deducted from payouts | CCTV on every zone; same-day COD deposit; separate cash box and ledger per carrier; UPI/QR payment straight to the carrier where offered |
| Exclusivity or conflict clauses | A contract forbids competing carriers on your premises | Read before signing; keep physically separate zones; disclose your other carriers upfront |
| Peak-season clashes | All big sales hit in Sep-Oct at once | Recruit temporary per-parcel riders from August; agree peak volume caps in writing |
| Rider churn and labour law | 10%+ monthly churn; per-parcel riders below minimum wage at low volume | Referral bonuses; PMSBY accident cover at ₹20 a year; keep per-rider volume above 45 a day; take CA or labour-law advice on contractor vs employee |
| Fraud | "Approval letter, now pay a fee" | Only official domains and area managers; never pay a personal account |
| Aggregator terms | Reselling under one account may break terms of service (not verified) | Ask Shiprocket or Ekart in writing; use the Ekart booking partner route, which allows resale |

## Verdict: multi-carrier works only as a patient, gated build

The multi-carrier approach is **better than a single-carrier hub**. The earlier analysis found a three-rider single-carrier hub losing about ₹14,000 a month in its base case. Spreading rent and staff over four carriers plus direct customers turns that into a business that pays its way by month 19. It works **only** under five conditions, all checkable before most of the money is committed.

| Condition | Why it matters (from the model) |
|---|---|
| 1. A written blended payout of **₹19 or more per delivered parcel**, ideally ₹20-21, with rider pay of ₹14.50 or less | At ₹17 the base case does not pay back within five years; at ₹21 it pays back in about 35 months |
| 2. **Two carriers from different parent groups live before the first festive season**, and no single carrier above 40% of revenue after month 12 | Ecom Express failed at 52% from one client; the base case reaches 31% at month 12 |
| 3. **Counter, seller shipping and fulfilment** providing at least half of contribution by month 24 | Last-mile alone cannot cover fixed costs; in the base case these streams give 66% |
| 4. **Access to about ₹11-12 lakh** in three tranches (₹5 lakh to start), or about ₹4 lakh if the optimistic rates are confirmed in writing | Most of the need is deposits and unpaid invoices that build up as you grow |
| 5. You **run the floor yourself** for the first year, and you **stop at Gate 1** if the numbers say stop | Saves about ₹3.3 lakh of peak cash and 13 months of payback; caps the downside at about ₹3 lakh |

If you cannot meet conditions 1 and 4, the better choice is the low-capital version: the counter plus aggregator booking plus Amazon Hub Delivery from a small shop, with no carrier hub at all.

## Conclusion

The common pitch for this business is "add more carriers, earn more". That gets the arithmetic wrong. More carriers mostly bring more parcels at the same thin spread, more deposits and more unpaid invoices to carry. What actually pays is using the same rented space and staff for several kinds of work: morning sorting for carriers, walk-in bookings by day, seller pickups in the afternoon, and pick-pack in year 2. That is also why the cash needed is set less by losses than by **working capital that grows with every new carrier**. A first-time owner should think of the ₹11.5 lakh base-case figure mostly as money waiting to be paid by carriers, plus deposits. It does not have to be all equity: a bank overdraft against receivables becomes realistic once you have 12 months of GST returns.

The deciding facts remain the same ones no carrier publishes: the real per-parcel rate, the PIN-code volume and the deposit. Every scenario in this report turns on those three numbers. Getting them in writing during the first 60 days, for about ₹20,000 of effort, is worth more than any later decision. Two ways to make the case clearly stronger are a rate of ₹21 and a hands-on owner. Two ways to break it are a rate of ₹17 and paying a deposit to an "agent".
